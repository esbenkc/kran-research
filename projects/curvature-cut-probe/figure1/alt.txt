Figure 1 asks: if a falsehood probe reads only output-sensitive directions, must a fine-tune that hides from it change the outputs more? Qwen2.5-1.5B reads the false statement 'The city of Durban is in Ukraine.' Two rows compare two probes on its activation at 'Ukraine'. The full probe reads all 1,536 directions. The cut probe reads only the 128 directions that move the next word most. On the original model both probes catch the statement. After a fine-tune trained to hide from each probe, both stay silent. The model's next word after 'Ukraine' barely changes in either case (in one fine-tune per probe: '.' 29% to 30%, ',' 17% to 18%, 'and' 14% to 13% or 12%). The price, the average change in the next-word prediction after the country over 1,192 test statements and 2 fine-tunes per probe, is 0.0054 for the full probe and 0.0048 for the cut probe. The answer is No: theory predicted 2.1 to 3.2 times the full probe's price, and we measured 0.9 times.
