The river begins as a thin stream among mossy stones up in the hills. Ferns lean over the water, and their fronds drip after every shower. Small brown trout hold still in the shade of the larger rocks. The water is cold and clear, and it tastes faintly of iron and earth. Above the stream, birch trees shed curls of white bark onto the path. Walkers often stop at the old stone bridge to listen to the water. The sound changes with the weather, from a soft murmur to a steady roar.

Lower down, the valley opens into wide meadows thick with grasses and wild flowers. Bees move slowly between clover heads and thistles. A heron stands at the edge of a shallow pool, patient and grey. When it strikes, it does so without warning, and the surface closes again right away. Swallows skim low over the meadow on warm afternoons. They catch insects in flight and return to nests under the eaves of a barn. The barn is built of dark timber, and its roof sags gently in the middle.

The soil here is deep and dark, rich with the remains of old leaves. Farmers in the past grew oats and barley on these slopes. These days, most of the fields are left for hay, and sheep graze on the higher ground. Dry stone walls divide the land into uneven shapes. Nobody remembers who built most of them. Lichens in pale green and orange cover the oldest stones. In places, a wall has fallen, and brambles have grown over the gap.

Morning fog often fills the valley floor and hides the river from view. The tops of the trees rise out of the mist like islands. As the sun climbs, the fog thins and drifts upward in slow ribbons. Dew hangs on spider webs strung between the stalks of tall grass. Each web catches the light and shines for a short while. By the middle of the morning, the air is clear, and the webs are almost invisible again.

Along the banks, alder and willow trees grow close to the water. Their roots reach into the stream and hold the soil in place. Otters sometimes use the hollows beneath these roots as shelter. They are shy animals and are seldom seen by day. Their tracks in the soft mud are the usual sign that they have come by. A kingfisher may flash by, a streak of bright blue just above the surface. It perches on a low branch and watches the water below.

The village sits at a bend in the river where the ground is level. Its houses are built of the same grey stone as the walls in the fields. Smoke rises from the chimneys on cold mornings. A small church with a square tower stands near the green. The churchyard is quiet, shaded by yew trees with dark needles. Rooks nest in the tall trees behind the tower and call loudly at dusk. Children play by the river on warm days, building dams of pebbles and sticks.

Bread is still baked in the village in a brick oven. The baker lights the fire long before dawn. The smell of warm crust drifts along the lane as people wake. Loaves are shaped by hand and left to rise in cloth lined baskets. The dough is mixed with flour from a mill further down the valley. The mill wheel no longer turns, but the building has been kept in good repair. Its walls are thick, and the spaces inside stay cool through the warmest weather.

The turn of the weather brings a change of colour to the whole valley. The birches turn yellow, and the bracken on the hillsides turns a deep rust. Mushrooms appear in the damp woods under the oaks. Some are small and pale, and others are broad caps of brown and gold. Geese fly overhead in long uneven lines, calling to each other. Their voices carry far in the still air. Leaves gather in the slower pools of the river and turn slowly in the eddies.

The cold months are quiet in the valley. Snow settles on the walls and outlines every stone. The river runs dark between white banks. Frost forms feathery patterns on the windows of the houses. Robins and wrens search the hedges for berries and seeds. On clear nights the stars are bright, because there are few lights nearby. The cold air smells clean and sharp. People walk carefully on the icy lanes and greet each other with a nod.

Warmth returns slowly, at the start as a softening of the air. Snowdrops push up through the cold ground under the hedges. Lambs appear in the fields and stay close to the ewes. The river swells with water from the melting snow on the hills. Buds on the willows open into pale catkins. Blackbirds sing from the rooftops in the long evening light. The meadows turn green again, and the early flowers open along the banks.

The woods on the eastern slope are older than the village. Oak, ash and holly grow there in a dense mixture. The floor of the wood is covered with bluebells for a short while as the weather warms. Their scent is sweet and heavy in the still air under the trees. Woodpeckers drum on dead branches, and the sound echoes between the trunks. Badgers dig their setts in the banks where the soil is soft. Their paths through the undergrowth are worn smooth by use.

A narrow lane climbs out of the valley toward the moor. It is lined with tall hedges of hawthorn and hazel. Late in the warm months the hedges are heavy with blackberries. Walkers pick them as they walk by and stain their fingers purple. Near the top of the lane, the hedges give way to open ground. The moor is wide and windswept, covered with heather and coarse grass. Curlews call over the moor, a long rising sound that seems to come from everywhere.

The heather flowers late in the warm months and turns the moor purple. Bees from the valley hives travel up to feed on it. Honey made from heather is dark and has a strong taste. Grouse rise suddenly from the cover with a loud whirr of wings. Small pools of peaty water lie in the hollows. Their surfaces reflect the clouds and the changing light. On a clear day, the view from the moor reaches far across the hills.

Weather on the moor can change quickly. A bright sky can turn grey within a short time as cloud rolls in from the west. Rain arrives in sheets, driven by the wind. Walkers learn to carry warm clothing even on fine days. When the cloud lifts, the light breaks through in moving patches. Sunlight sweeps across the hillsides like a lamp carried by a giant. Then the moor shines wet and bright, and the air smells of earth and heather.

Stone markers stand at intervals along the old tracks across the moor. They were placed long ago to guide travellers in poor weather. Some lean at odd angles, and some have fallen into the heather. Moss and lichen have softened their outlines. Few people use these tracks anymore, except walkers and shepherds. The tracks are still clear on the ground, worn by countless feet. They wind between the hills and drop into the next valley.

The next valley is narrower and steeper than the last. Its river runs fast over a bed of large boulders. Waterfalls break the stream into white steps. The spray keeps the rocks wet and green with moss. Dippers bob on the stones and walk under the water to feed. Rowan trees cling to the sides of the gorge, their roots gripping the cracks. Their berries turn bright red as the warm months end.

At the foot of the gorge, the river widens into a small lake. The lake is fringed with reeds that rustle in the wind. Coots and moorhens move in and out of the reeds. Swans glide across the open water in pairs. In calm weather, the surface is like glass and holds a perfect image of the hills. Anglers sit on the bank for long hours in silence. The water is deep in the middle and dark even on bright days.

A path follows the shore of the lake through a belt of pine trees. The ground under the pines is soft with fallen needles. The air there is resinous and warm. Red squirrels leap between the branches and scold walkers from above. Crossbills work at the cones with their twisted beaks. Light falls through the canopy in narrow shafts. The path leaves the pines at a small beach of coarse sand and pebbles.

From the beach, the lake stretches away toward the far hills. Small islands rise from the water, each crowned with a few trees. Cormorants dry their wings on the rocks of the nearest island. Their dark shapes are still for long periods. Wind ripples move across the water in fans. When the wind drops, the lake falls silent, and only the birds can be heard. Evening light turns the water gold and then deep blue.

The valley people tell stories about the lake and the moor. Some say a bell can be heard under the water on still nights. Others speak of a lost path that crosses the moor in fog. Most of these stories are told with a smile. They are part of the place, like the stones and the trees. Visitors hear them in the inn by the fire on cold evenings. The stories change a little with every telling, and nobody minds.

The inn stands at the crossing of the lanes, close to the old bridge. Its beams are low and black with age, and its floors slope a little. A fire burns in the wide hearth on most evenings. Dogs sleep in front of it, stretched out on the warm stones. Walkers dry their boots by the fire and talk about the weather on the hills. The landlord keeps a heavy book where travellers write about their walks. The book is filled with sketches of birds, notes about paths and small pressed flowers.

Behind the inn, a walled garden slopes gently down to the river. Old apple trees grow along its walls, their branches trained flat against the stone. Their blossom is white and pink, and it falls like snow when the wind rises. Beds of herbs fill the middle of the garden with thyme, sage and mint. Butterflies settle on the lavender and open their wings in the sun. A wooden bench faces the water, worn smooth by long use. From the bench, the river can be heard but not quite seen through the willows.

Night comes slowly to the valley in the warm months. The sky fades from blue to violet, and the hills turn into dark outlines. Bats leave the church tower and hunt over the water. Owls call from the woods, a soft hoot answered from further away. Moths gather around the lamp over the door of the inn. The river keeps its steady sound in the dark. Little by little, the windows of the village turn dark, and the valley sleeps.

Before dawn, the valley is grey and still. A thrush starts to sing from the top of a hawthorn, and a robin answers from the hedge below. Mist lies on the water like a pale sheet. The cows in the lower meadow stand close together, their breath steaming in the cool air. A fox crosses the field along the line of the wall, unhurried and alert. It pauses at the gate, looks back, and slips into the wood. The sky behind the eastern hills turns slowly from grey to rose.

The weir below the village holds back a long, calm pool. Water slides over its lip in a smooth, glassy curve and breaks into foam below. Young boys fish from the stones beside it with simple rods. Sand martins dig their burrows in the steep bank above the pool. They fly in and out so fast that the eye can hardly keep up with them. On hot days, cattle wade into the shallows to cool their legs. Dragonflies patrol the reeds and hover over the water with a dry rattle of wings.

An old track runs along the river below the weir, between the water and a row of poplars. The poplars are tall and narrow, and their leaves shiver in the slightest breeze. The track is soft with grass, and it is pleasant to walk barefoot there. Wild garlic grows in the damp ground under the trees. Its white flowers shine in the shade, and its smell fills the air. Wood pigeons clap their wings as they rise from the branches. Further on, the track crosses a little stream by a plank bridge with a narrow rail.

A potter works in a small shed near the plank bridge. She digs her clay from the riverbank and leaves it to weather in heaps. Her wheel turns with a quiet hum, and her hands shape bowls and jugs with slow care. The glazes are made from ash and local stone. When the kiln is fired, smoke drifts over the water for a whole day and night. The finished pots are the colours of the valley, brown and green and the grey of wet slate. Each piece is a little uneven, and she likes them that way.

Rain in the valley often comes softly and lasts for hours. It patters on the leaves and gathers in the ruts of the lanes. The river rises and turns the colour of strong tea. Puddles reflect the low sky, and the hills disappear behind veils of cloud. Snails leave silver trails across the stones of the garden walls. Indoors, people mend nets, read by the window and listen to the rain on the roof. When the rain stops at last, every leaf drips, and the birds begin to sing again right away.

After heavy rain, the waterfalls in the gorge become loud and wild. The spray drifts far across the path and soaks anyone who stands near. The pools below boil with white water, and logs tumble through the rapids. The noise fills the narrow valley and drowns out every other sound. Within a few days, the flood falls back, and the river runs clear again. Fresh gravel lies spread across the bends where the water broke its banks. Herons return to the shallows and stand as if nothing has happened.

Late in the day, long shadows stretch across the meadows from the line of the hills. The light turns warm and low, and every blade of grass seems lit from within. Swifts scream as they race around the church tower in tight circles. Cattle walk slowly toward the gate where they will be milked. A breeze stirs the tops of the poplars and then dies away. The smell of cut hay drifts from a field where the grass has been turned to dry. Somewhere across the river, a dog barks and a door closes, and then the valley is quiet again.
